What makes a copepod population grow or shrink?

Copepods are tiny, shrimp-like animals, each about the size of a grain of rice, that drift through the ocean. They eat microscopic algae and are eaten by fish, so they are a key link in the ocean food web. Change the water and food below and watch how a model copepod population responds over the years.

How the model works

The life of a copepod

A copepod starts as an egg. The egg hatches into a larva called a nauplius, which grows through five juvenile stages (called C1 to C5) and finally becomes an adult. Adult females lay eggs, and the cycle repeats. At every step, some copepods are eaten by predators or starve.

The model follows groups of eggs laid on different days of the year and tracks how big they get, how much energy they store, and how many survive. It then adds up all the groups to draw the population graph. A population survives when, on average, each mother leaves behind more than one surviving daughter that grows up to lay eggs of her own.

Two tricks help Calanus copepods survive harsh seasons. They store fat (lipids) while algae are plentiful, and in the food-poor season, juveniles can go into a dormant resting state called diapause. They sink to deep, cold water, stop eating and slow their metabolism to a quarter of normal, living on the stored fat. The model includes both.

Evidence behind each slider

Temperature. Cold-blooded animals speed up when it is warm. In the model, development speeds up about 3 times for every 10 °C of warming and growth about 2.5 times (Forster et al. 2011; Campbell et al. 2001). A warmer copepod also burns energy faster, so it needs more food. Warm water has another effect on eggs: in lab tests, Calanus finmarchicus eggs hatched fine up to 19 °C, fewer than 15% hatched at 21 °C, and none hatched at 24 °C or above (Preziosi 2012). The model applies this, so you will only see it at the top of the temperature slider. Experiments in Greenland also showed that egg production of C. finmarchicus more than tripled between 2.5 and 5 °C, while C. glacialis was already laying eggs at 0 to 2.5 °C (Kjellerup et al. 2012).

Food. Food is the fuel. In the model, eating, growing and egg laying rise with the amount of algae and level off when the copepod is full (the Michaelis–Menten curve, with half-full at 1 mg chlorophyll per m³). The timing of the bloom matters as much as its size: if eggs hatch long before or after the bloom, the babies starve (Varpe et al. 2007; Banas et al. 2016).

pH (acidity). The ocean is slowly becoming more acidic as it absorbs carbon dioxide (CO₂), so scientists have tested copepods at low pH. The results are reassuring for Calanus. Eggs, larvae and egg laying were not harmed at levels forecast for the year 2300 (Bailey et al. 2017), and egg production was unchanged down to pH 6.9, although hatching was delayed (Weydmann et al. 2012). In cold water, eggs hatched normally down to about pH 6.6. In warm water (15 °C), hatching dropped at about pH 7.0 and below (Preziosi et al. 2017). The model therefore only lowers hatching, and only at low pH, with a bigger effect in warmer water. Other copepods can be more sensitive. For the small copepod Acartia tonsa, hatching fell by more than 20% at forecast 2100 CO₂ levels when both parents had been exposed (Cripps et al. 2014).

Salinity (saltiness). Calanus copepods are surprisingly tough. In a Greenland study, feeding rates did not change between salinities of 25 and 40, but about half of the animals died at salinities near 9 (C. glacialis) or 14 (C. finmarchicus) (Makri et al. 2024). Melting ice and river runoff can drop the salinity of Arctic surface water that low. The model adds harm starting below a salinity of 25, reaching its maximum at 10.

Predators. Each day a fixed share of copepods is eaten. Bigger copepods and warmer water make this share larger, following global measurements by Hirst and Kiørboe (2002).

How far should you trust this? (limits of the model)

This is a simplified model, built on real experiments, but it is a tool for exploring ideas, not a forecast.

The temperature, food, growth, and predation parts come directly from a published model (Banas et al. 2016), which was tested against real populations. The pH and salinity responses are mine. The direction and thresholds come from the experiments above, but the exact size of the harm at extreme values is an assumption. At the lowest salinities the model doubles the death rate, cuts egg laying by 60% and cuts hatching by 70%; at the lowest pH it cuts hatching by up to 60%. Because small changes in survival add up over a copepod’s multi-year life, even these moderate changes make the population collapse below a salinity of about 20. These are plausible, not measured, values. Most tests lasted days to weeks, not whole life cycles.

The model describes cold-water, ocean-living Calanus copepods. Tropical copepods, or estuary species that live in brackish water (such as Acartia tonsa or Eurytemora affinis, which hatch best at low salinities; Kuismanen et al. 2020), would respond very differently. The food bloom is a smooth idealized curve, the temperature cycle is fixed at ±2.5 °C around your average, and food quality, disease, ocean currents and multiple generations of acid exposure are not included. The crowding limit of 3000 animals per m² is my own addition to stop the graph growing forever.

For the curious: technical details

Each cohort of eggs spawned on one date is tracked with four state variables: developmental stage D (0 = egg, 1 = adult), structural mass S, energy reserves R and survivorship N. Development and ingestion follow a Michaelis–Menten response to food (half-saturation 1 mg chl m⁻³) and power-law (Q₁₀) responses to temperature (Q₁₀ = 3.0 for development, 2.5 for growth). Ingestion, metabolism and predation scale with body mass to the power 0.7. After stage C3 a copepod enters diapause when food falls below a threshold: it stops feeding and developing, metabolism drops to one quarter, and it sits at deep-water temperature (0.4 × mean surface temperature). Cohorts starve if reserves fall below −10% of structure. Adults produce eggs from income (current feeding) and capital (stored lipid) once the egg-production start date has passed. Cohorts from every spawning date (5-day steps) are run for up to 3 years, assembled into a transition matrix, and projected forward day by day. The u₀ trait (development rate at 0 °C) sets adult size and life-cycle length.

pH harm x = clamp((7.2 − pH)/0.6) × (0.4 + 0.6·clamp((T − 3)/12)); hatching × (1 − 0.6x), mortality × (1 + 0.1x). Salinity harm y = clamp((25 − S)/15)²; mortality × (1 + y), ingestion × (1 − 0.3y), egg production × (1 − 0.6y), hatching × (1 − 0.7y). Thermal hatching factor: 1 up to 19 °C, 0.15 at 21 °C, 0 at 24 °C and above (linear between).
